1. What Is Research Really About?

Academic research is not simply “choose a model, run a dataset, and report an accuracy.” The core of research is to ask a worthwhile question, design evidence that can answer it, and state honestly how far that evidence supports your conclusion.

QuestionWhat do you truly want to know? Is the question specific, measurable, and falsifiable?
EvidenceWhat data or experiments could distinguish between competing answers?
ControlWhat baseline or control is needed to determine where an improvement actually comes from?
ReproducibilityCould another researcher reconstruct the main result from your methods, data, and code?
InterpretationWhat do the results support, and what conclusions go beyond the evidence?
CommunicationCan a reader understand the question, method, key evidence, and limitations within a few minutes?
In one sentence: Research is a chain of justified decisions. Every “why did you do it this way?” should have a scientific reason—not merely “because the code runs.”

2. The Complete Research Workflow

Observation / Problem ↓ Narrow research question ↓ Literature review ↓ Research gap + contribution ↓ Hypothesis / objective ↓ Study protocol + baselines + metrics ↓ Data collection / dataset construction ↓ Analysis / model development ↓ Validation + robustness + error analysis ↓ Interpretation + limitations ↓ Paper / report / presentation ↓ Data + code + metadata + reproducibility package

ContributionIf the study succeeds, what new knowledge will it add?

4. Literature Review: Do Not Just Collect PDFs—Map What Is Known, Unknown, and Contested

The most useful output of a literature review is not a pile of summaries. It is a research map showing the major approaches, datasets, findings, limitations, and unresolved questions.

A three-pass reading strategy

Pass 1 — 5 minutesTitle, abstract, figures, and conclusion. Decide whether the paper deserves deeper reading.
Pass 2 — 20–40 minutesResearch question, dataset, methods, main results, and limitations.
Pass 3 — Deep readingReconstruct the experimental logic, inspect assumptions, trace key citations, and judge reproducibility.

Record at least these 10 fields for every important paper

FieldWhat to record
CitationAuthors, year, DOI / URL
QuestionThe actual question the paper addresses
DatasetSource, scale, inclusion and exclusion criteria
Split / validationTraining / validation / test / external validation
MethodCore model and critical parameters
BaselineWhether the comparison is fair and appropriate
Primary metricHow the main result is evaluated
Main resultThe key quantitative result or effect
LimitationWhat the authors admit—and what you additionally notice
RelevanceHow this paper changes your research design

Research gaps come in many forms

Knowledge gapMethod gapDataset gap Generalization gapMechanistic gapReplication gap Clinical / translational gapReporting gap
Weak novelty claim: “Nobody has applied model X to dataset Y.”
Stronger framing: Identify an important condition under which current approaches fail, then explain why your design could address that failure.

6. Research Standards Especially Important in Machine Learning and Bioinformatics

Computational research can produce attractive numbers very easily. Attractive numbers are not the same as reliable science. The following practices should become routine.

IssueMinimum expectation
Dataset provenanceDocument where the data came from, when it was downloaded, the version, and cleaning rules.
LeakageEnsure that duplicate subjects, near-duplicate samples, homologous sequences, patients, or time-dependent information do not leak across splits.
BaselineInclude simple and reasonable baselines—not only complex models.
Controlled experimentIf studying kernel size, do not simultaneously change optimizer, filter count, and augmentation.
Hyperparameter selectionSelect using validation data only; use the independent test set at the end.
MetricsChoose metrics that match the problem; for imbalanced data, do not rely only on accuracy or ROC-AUC.
Repeated runsIf results are sensitive to initialization, use multiple random seeds and report mean ± SD / CI.
AblationRemove key components to determine what actually causes the improvement.
Error analysisDo not report only average scores; inspect false positives, false negatives, and hard cases.
External validationIf you claim generalization, evaluate on data from another source, time period, or institution when possible.
Compute reportReport software version, GPU/CPU, epochs, batch size, seeds, and runtime.
A better ML research question: Not “my model achieved 0.95 AUC,” but “does the improvement remain after leakage control, independent testing, fixed baselines, and predefined metrics?”

8. Results Should Answer the Research Question—not Dump Every Output

For each major result, use Claim → Evidence → Limitation

ClaimEvidenceLimitation / boundary
What did you observe?Which figure, table, or number directly supports it?For which data, conditions, or populations does it hold?

Results and Discussion serve different purposes

ResultsWhat happened? Report predefined and important findings systematically.
DiscussionWhat does it mean? Connect to prior work, explain mechanisms, discuss limitations, and interpret implications.

Evaluate results along at least four dimensions

effect / magnitudeuncertainty robustnesspractical / biological meaning
Avoid: declaring an important breakthrough solely because p<0.05 or one metric improved by 0.003. Ask how large the effect is, how stable it is, and whether it survives different splits, seeds, or external datasets.

9. Academic Writing: Use IMRaD to Build a Traceable Argument

Empirical research often follows IMRaD: Introduction, Methods, Results, and Discussion. The EQUATOR Network provides reporting guidelines for many study types, including randomized trials, observational studies, systematic reviews, prediction models, and diagnostic studies.

What the reader wants from each section

SectionReader's questionCommon beginner mistake
TitleWhat did you study?Too broad, vague, or only a model name.
AbstractWhat was the problem, method, key result, and meaning?Too much background, no core quantitative result.
IntroductionWhy is this study needed?Reads like a textbook and never identifies the gap.
MethodsWhat exactly did you do, and can I reproduce it?Missing split rules, versions, parameters, or exclusion criteria.
ResultsWhat did you actually find?Mixing interpretation into the Results section.
DiscussionWhat do the findings mean in relation to existing knowledge?Repeating results or overclaiming.
LimitationsWhere should I stop trusting the conclusion?One token sentence with no real boundary conditions.
ConclusionWhat is the most important evidence-supported takeaway?Presenting future work as if it were completed evidence.

A four-step Introduction

1. Important problem / context ↓ 2. What is already known ↓ 3. What remains unresolved (gap) ↓ 4. What this study asks / contributes

The standard for Methods

PLOS author guidance treats Methods as the backbone of credibility and replicability: the description should be detailed enough for a researcher with similar training to reproduce the work, not merely identify the model name.

A practical writing order

Figures / tables ↓ Results ↓ Methods ↓ Discussion ↓ Introduction ↓ Abstract ↓ Title

This is not a mandatory rule. It is a useful beginner workflow because it forces the paper to grow from the evidence that actually exists rather than from an overambitious story.

10. Figures: Every Figure Should Answer a Question

A good figure is not decoration. It is a piece of evidence. Write the figure's message first, then decide what panels are needed to support that message.

FigureTypical function
Fig. 1Research question / workflow / dataset construction
Fig. 2Method architecture and experimental design
Fig. 3Main performance / primary endpoint
Fig. 4Ablation / controlled experiments
Fig. 5Robustness / external validation
Fig. 6Error analysis / biological interpretation
Figure self-test: If you remove the main text and leave only the figure plus caption, can the reader still identify what was compared, what the main result is, and what the sample size / uncertainty means?

11. Preparing an Academic Presentation: Do Not Paste the Paper into PowerPoint

Harvard Catalyst emphasizes slide design that helps audiences understand and remember scientific information. MIT Communication Lab recommends one message per slide and keeping only information that supports that message. Nature's presentation guidance similarly emphasizes building a research story around a key message.

A practical 20-slide research presentation

1. TitleTitle, name, affiliation, one-sentence topic statement.
2. Big problemThe important problem in the field.
3. Why it mattersScientific / clinical / engineering importance.
4. What we knowOnly the background needed to understand the study.
5. GapThe unresolved limitation in current knowledge or methods.
6. Research questionOne slide for the central question / hypothesis.
7. DatasetSource, scale, quality, and split.
8. Study designControl / baseline / validation strategy.
9. Method overviewOne clear workflow diagram.
10. Technical methodOnly the technical details needed to understand the results.
11. EvaluationPrimary metrics and success criteria.
12. BaselineEstablish the reference point.
13. Main resultThe single most important piece of evidence.
14. Controlled / ablationWhere did the improvement come from?
15. RobustnessDifferent seeds / subsets / conditions.
16. Independent validationTruly unseen data.
17. Error analysisWhere and why does the method fail?
18. InterpretationHow should the result change our understanding?
19. LimitationsDefine the boundary of trust explicitly.
20. Take-home messageNo more than three conclusions + Q&A.

Assertion–Evidence principle

Weak title: “Results”
Stronger title: “Pretraining improved independent-test MCC, but gains disappeared without homology control.”

The slide title states the claim; the figure provides the evidence.

12. How to Deliver the Academic Presentation

Define the audience firstA specialist faculty audience, interdisciplinary researchers, and students require different levels of background.
Keep only 1–3 core messagesNature's presentation guidance repeatedly emphasizes key messages and research story.
Do not read the slidesThe slides should carry visual evidence; you provide the explanation.
Problem before toolDo not begin by introducing CNNs, Transformers, or software. Begin with the research problem.
Rehearse with a timerDeleting material is usually better than speaking faster.
Prepare for Q&AAnticipate questions about datasets, baselines, leakage, statistics, and limitations.

Every results slide can follow three steps

Question ↓ Evidence (figure / table) ↓ One-sentence interpretation

13. In 2026, AI Can Assist Research—but Responsibility Cannot Be Outsourced

Current ICMJE guidance states that AI-assisted technologies should not be listed as authors. Human authors remain responsible for accuracy, citations, originality, and the final content, and AI use should be disclosed appropriately. A 2026 Nature Computational Science editorial likewise emphasizes human oversight, accountability, and transparency. If AI materially contributes to data analysis, figure generation, or the research workflow, describe that contribution clearly in Methods or another required disclosure section.

Reasonable uses of AI

Literature explorationGenerate search terms and organize concepts, then verify claims in the original sources.
ProgrammingGenerate boilerplate, debug code, and write tests—but understand and verify the code yourself.
Writing supportImprove language and structure, but do not allow AI to invent data, citations, or conclusions.
Analysis assistanceAI may suggest analyses, but assumptions, code, and numerical outputs require independent verification.

Minimum AI-use record

RecordWhat to document
ToolTool / model name and version, if available
PurposeLiterature search, code, language editing, analysis, figure generation, etc.
Human verificationHow the output was checked
Research impactWhether AI materially changed the method or analysis
DisclosureWhere it was disclosed according to journal / institutional rules
Unacceptable: fabricated citations, invented data, unverified analysis, or uploading confidential manuscripts / patient data to an unauthorized external AI system.

14. Twelve Common Beginner Research Mistakes

#MistakeCorrection
1The topic is too broadConvert it into one measurable research question.
2Choosing a fancy model before identifying the problemDefine the gap, data, and outcome first.
3Repeatedly looking at the test setOpen the test set only after model selection is complete.
4Changing many parameters at the same timeUse controlled experiments or an explicit factorial design.
5Reporting only the best runUse multiple seeds and report uncertainty.
6Reporting only accuracyChoose metrics appropriate to the problem.
7Using weak baselinesInclude simple, established, and relevant strong baselines.
8Treating literature review as a collection of summariesOrganize evidence, disagreement, limitations, and gaps.
9Writing an incomplete Methods sectionWrite enough detail for a domain peer to reproduce the study.
10Treating results as conclusionsSeparate observation from interpretation.
11Filling slides with paper textUse one message per slide and visual evidence.
12Using AI output without verificationVerify, document, disclose, and retain human accountability.
